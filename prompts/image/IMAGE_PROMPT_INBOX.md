Pripyat, the abandoned Soviet nuclear town in the Chernobyl exclusion zone, photographed today. This is a
real, specific building — the real one, as it stands now.

Camera optical centre 1.8 metres above the square, turned about thirty-five degrees off the
face of the building so the façade runs diagonally away from the left of frame toward a vanishing point in
the right third — never square on, never symmetrical, never a flat elevation.

THE BUILDING, exactly as it is. A long low horizontal modernist block, three levels, pale grey-beige
concrete and stone cladding gone chalky and streaked.
Its defining feature is the façade of the upper two floors: a long regular row of TALL SLENDER VERTICAL
CONCRETE FINS — thin flat ribs, not round columns and not a classical portico — standing proud of a deeply
recessed wall behind them. There are around twenty of them across the length of the building, evenly
spaced, running the full height of those two floors. The recessed wall behind was fully glazed and is now
a row of empty black openings, some with bent aluminium frames still hanging.
Below the fins, a long horizontal terrace slab runs the whole width of the building with a rusted steel
railing along it, brown-orange with rust and broken away in places. Under the terrace the ground floor sits
back in deep shadow, its entrance openings black.
The RIGHT end of the building is a taller solid windowless block clad in large pale panels, plain and flat,
stepping up above the line of fins.
On the roof parapet, standing free against the sky, large detached metal letters spelling "Энергетик" in a
slanted script, each letter fixed on its own bracket, all of them heavily rusted to orange-brown. Along the
parapet to the left of them, smaller upright block letters spelling "ДВОРЕЦ КУЛЬТУРЫ", pale and chipped.
Both signs are corroded, unlit and half-ruined, never repainted.

THE OVERGROWTH. Poplar and birch saplings four to eight metres tall stand right up against the façade and
grow out of the terrace itself, their crowns crossing in front of the fins. Dense green scrub fills the
